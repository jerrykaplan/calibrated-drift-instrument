The water cycle describes how water moves between the oceans, the atmosphere, and the land. Energy from the sun heats the surface of oceans, lakes, and rivers, and some of the water evaporates into the air as vapor. Plants also release water vapor through small openings in their leaves, a process called transpiration. As warm, moist air rises, it expands and cools. Cooler air can hold less water vapor, so some of the vapor condenses onto tiny particles of dust, salt, or smoke, forming the droplets that make up clouds. When droplets collide and grow large enough, they fall as precipitation: rain, snow, sleet, or hail, depending on the temperature of the air they pass through. Some precipitation lands directly in the oceans. Some falls on land, where it may soak into the soil and recharge groundwater stored in porous rock layers called aquifers. Other water runs across the surface into streams and rivers, which eventually carry it back to the sea. In cold regions, water can remain frozen in glaciers and ice sheets for thousands of years before melting. Groundwater can also travel slowly underground and emerge in springs or seep into riverbeds. The total amount of water on Earth stays nearly constant, but its distribution changes with the seasons and over longer periods of climate change. Scientists measure rainfall, river flow, soil moisture, and evaporation to build models of the cycle. These models help communities plan reservoirs, manage irrigation for farms, forecast floods and droughts, and protect the quality of drinking water supplies for growing cities.
